Lean Product Design Playbook

Introduction

Lean product design is an approach to building products that encompasses a set of ideas about collaboration, design, and project management. Doing lean product design means fundamentally changing your focus from outputs (for example, features, functionality, colors) to outcomes (the changes in user behavior you want to see).

At the core of lean product design is hypothesis-driven development. Many of our beliefs about users, their problems, and possible solutions are assumptions that need to be tested. We can do this by building in small, iterative pieces with the goal of learning whether our assumptions are well-founded — and ultimately whether our ideas or features will have the outcomes we want, both for the business and for our end users.

Why use a lean approach?

When building digital tools and services, it's tempting to think in terms of features. But our users don't care about what features our sites have; they care what they can accomplish. This is also true of the people and agencies who own the sites: while they may talk about features, they actually care more about whether the site helps them achieve their mission and business goals.

Lean product design keeps us focused on outcomes and forces us to check, as we build, how our product works and what it accomplishes.

Who it's for and how to use it

This guide is designed to help product teams work together by establishing a shared vocabulary, set of principles, and basic process. There are two main parts:

  1. Principles: Frameworks for thinking about product design and process, serving as a reference throughout.
  2. Process: Nine steps that form the foundation for working iteratively while staying grounded in user outcomes.

Note: This guide focuses on using scrum and agile processes for lean product design.

Credit and thanks: Many of these ideas are based on works by Jeff Gothelf, Josh Seiden, and Eric Ries.

Principles

Outcomes, not outputs

Focus on outcomes — the changes in user behavior you want to see — rather than specific features.

Learn and respond

Think of your project as a series of experiments in close collaboration with users. Each experiment informs the next.

Cross-disciplinary teams

Teams should be cross-disciplinary, closely integrated, and include the product owner as an active design participant.

Build shared understanding

The entire team participates in all activities together, from planning and problem framing to research, design, and execution.

Make assumptions explicit

Make your assumptions explicit, and test the riskiest or most critical assumptions as early as possible.

Keep experiments light

Move quickly and don't build too much investment in a particular solution before validating it.

Prioritization is your friend

Prioritization is the (not so) secret weapon to keep your efforts lean and focused.

Always think problem first

Always start a project by taking a fresh look at the problem(s) and doing some discovery.

Lean Product Design PlaybookPrinciples
Step One

Conduct Discovery Research

The first stage of any project is to do research to discover problems that need solving. These design methods can help guide your research with both stakeholders and possible end users:

  • Contextual inquiry
  • Bodystorming
  • Stakeholder and user interviews
  • Journey mapping
  • Cognitive walkthroughs
  • KJ method
  • Heuristic analysis

Your goal is three-fold:

  1. Identify and more deeply understand the challenge facing the organization and its stakeholders;
  2. Identify the people you believe could be most helped by your solution; and,
  3. Explore the problem, context, behaviors, and motivations of the people (your intended users).

Example: The challenge — the U.S. has high unemployment and growing demand for skilled workers. The people — high school graduates and adults without a degree. The problem — prospective college students lack information about potential economic outcomes of a degree.

Lean product design may change how you do UX research. Iteration and learning over repeated experiments is valued over breadth of research. Document results clearly and concisely so you can share them quickly, learn from them, and devise another experiment.

Step Two

Write a Problem Statement

Gather your research and use it to create a problem statement. Describe ways that you will know (or measure) when you have solved the problem.

Write your project's statement in the following format:

We have observed that [product/service/organization] isn't meeting [these goals/needs], which is causing [this adverse effect]. How might we improve so that our product/service/team/organization is more successful based on [these measurable criteria]?

If you have trouble writing a narrow problem statement, brainstorm all the project's possible goals, needs, and measurable criteria first. Then work as a group to select the most important ones and build your problem statement from there.

This process may reveal multiple problem statements. Your team will need to figure out which problem to pursue, and how to scope the statement so there are some constraints around what the team is taking on.

Step Three

Identify Your Assumptions

Your problem and ideas about how to solve it are based on a set of assumptions. Lean product design is all about surfacing and testing those assumptions. Before diving into building hypotheses, it's important to consider all of your project's potential assumptions.

Thinking about your problem statement, map out all the assumptions you have about needs, users, and potential solutions. Everyone should contribute, and no ideas should be rejected.

Needs and Solution Assumptions

  1. I believe my users have a need to:
  2. I believe these needs can be solved with:
  3. I believe the #1 value a user wants to get out of my service is:
  4. I believe the user can also get these additional benefits:
  5. I believe I will acquire the majority of my users through:
  6. I believe my biggest product risk is:
  7. We will solve this through:
  8. What systems will our solution need to interact with?
  9. What other assumptions do we have that, if proven false, will cause our project to fail?

User Assumptions

  1. Who is the user?
  2. What problems does our product solve?
  3. When and how is our product used?
  4. What features are important?
  5. How should our product look and behave?
  6. What are our design principles?
  7. What will the touchpoints be?
Lean Product Design PlaybookStep 3: Identify Assumptions
Step Four

Select Assumptions

Now we select the assumptions we need to test. Identify assumptions that meet one or more of the following conditions:

Core assumptions: Any assumptions that must be true for your solution to fix the problem.

Unknown assumptions: The ones your team is most unsure about — in order to learn quickly about possible risks.

Risky assumptions: Assumptions that — if proven wrong — would cause the project to fail.

With your team, read through your assumptions and highlight those that are core, unknown, or risky. Tip: It may be helpful to have each team member do this activity individually, then come together for discussion.

Step Seven

Break Down Hypotheses

You may have a mix of hypotheses: some call for building or prototyping software, and some may call for non-software actions. It's important to break all hypotheses down into more specific, actionable sub-hypotheses that can be tracked in your project.

Broad hypothesis: "We believe that building a website will result in better citizen engagement. We'll know we're right when we receive more contacts from citizens."

Broken down: "We believe that creating a way for citizens to easily contact our bureau from the homepage will result in better citizen engagement. We'll know we're right when our daily contact volume goes up at least 10 percent."

Broad hypotheses should be broken down enough so that individual team members have a good starting point for generating solution ideas, but general enough that it isn't too prescriptive.

Good: "A way for constituents to easily contact their senators."

Not good (too prescriptive): "A text input field with a button people can click that sends their message to their senator."

Break your hypotheses down into more specific sub-hypotheses. Bring them into your story-tracking board as cards in a "story candidates" list. Keep your broad hypotheses somewhere the team can refer back to them.

Step Nine

Plan the Sprint & Kick Off the Agile Cycle

Lean is the "brain" in the agile process. Agile on its own is good at managing delivery, but it does not help you ensure that you're building the right thing. Lean acts as the "brain": everything you build in a sprint is an experiment — with the focus on learning whether it gets you the outcome you expected. The team and client must be willing to adjust, pivot, or discard, or it isn't really an experiment.

Before the sprint

1. Groom the backlog

Who: Product owner, project lead, team optional.
What: Order hypotheses (stories) by priority in the appropriate backlog.

At the beginning of the sprint

2. Plan the sprint

Who: All! (PO/PM/Team)
What: Bring stories into the sprint backlog. Team defines and volunteers for tasks, and writes acceptance criteria.

3. Hold a design studio

Who: All! Including PO. Everyone must sketch.
What: Iterate through ideas, allow input from the entire team, build shared understanding, and break down hypotheses.

During the sprint

4. Build

As work starts, move tasks to a "working" list. When completed, move to "acceptance testing." Define acceptance criteria as a mix of basic functionality and desired user outcomes.

5. Research

Who: Include team, POs whenever possible.
What: Test features with users. Conduct research that tests current sprint hypotheses and explores questions for future sprints.

At the end of the sprint

6. Synthesize research results

Who: Include team, POs whenever possible.
What: Ask: what did we learn? Do we need to adjust? Capture insights into the "story candidates backlog" for future prioritization.

7. Hold a sprint review

Who: The whole team, and any stakeholders.
What: Demo completed stories. Review key findings: What was learned? What hypotheses were proved or disproved? What will we try next?

What's next?

Based on what the team learned, you may need new hypotheses, or you may move on to the next set of priority stories. Repeat the sprint cycle, always with the focus on learning. The project lead and product owner must ensure the sprint hypotheses feed back up to inform the broad outcomes and goals.
